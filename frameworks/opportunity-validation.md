# Opportunity Validation Framework

**Use:** Evaluating new market opportunities and producing a go/no-go recommendation. Distinct from the Business Fundamentals Canvas (which maps an existing business model), this framework is a *process* for testing a hypothesis before committing resources.

---

## Core Principle

**Simplicity.** Always reduce to the most essential question. Complexity is a sign you haven't gotten to the bottom of the problem yet.

---

## The Process

### 1. Frame the Hypothesis
State the business case clearly. What are you betting is true? Keep it short enough to challenge.

### 2. Map What You Know vs. Don't Know
Be explicit about both. An unspoken assumption is more dangerous than a wrong one, it diverges across people and never gets tested. Surface every assumption, even the ones that feel obvious.

### 3. Identify "What Must Be True"
List every condition that must hold for this opportunity to be viable. Don't skip the uncomfortable ones.

### 4. Prioritize the Most Worrying Condition
Of all the things that must be true, which one are you most worried about? That's where to focus first. Everything else is secondary until the critical uncertainty is resolved.

### 5. Raise Questions Before Collecting Data
Don't start fieldwork until you know what you're trying to learn. Data collection is expensive, questions direct where it goes. Undefined questions lead to unfocused data.

### 6. Validate the Question Set
Before going to the field, pressure-test your questions and assumptions with the right people. Are these the right things to learn? Are the assumptions reasonable starting points? Then go.

### 7. Collect Data
At early stages: conversations, not quantitative data. Talk to the people closest to the problem. Patterns will emerge.

For each signal, record who said it, their role, and the context. A technical estimate from an internal expert is not the same as a commercial deliverable request from a buyer. The difference is only visible if the source and context are preserved. Signal attribution is what keeps awareness from being mistaken for demand.

### 8. Go / No-Go
Return to "what must be true." Which conditions hold? Which are broken? Make the call.

### 9. Communicate the Recommendation
The explanation is as important as the conclusion, it educates others and prevents the same question from resurfacing. A no-go recommendation should be short (1-2 pages), not a data dump. Include a simple illustration if the concept isn't obvious to a non-domain reader. Distill the evidence, reference the full investigation for anyone who wants depth.

For a management audience: lead with the revenue logic that matters most to them (e.g., new installations driving downstream consumable revenue), not with the software or feature-level opportunity. Match the framing to the audience's priorities.

---

## Key Principles

**Start with the customer.**
Break the customer population into categories. Paint a picture simple enough to hold in your head. If you can't describe the person who would "grab you by the shoulders" when they see your product, you don't know your customer yet.

**Demand fit, not supply fill.**
When framing assumptions, check which direction you're reasoning from. Supply framing starts from an asset or capability: "we have X, how do we fill it?" Demand framing starts from the customer's workflow: "does this customer have a specific problem where this solution is actually the right tool?"

Supply framing is a trap because idle capacity does not equal fit. A customer can have an underutilized asset and still have no suitable work to route through your solution. The question is never "is there a gap we could fill?", it's "is there a job-to-be-done that belongs here?"

*Example:* A logistics company has trucks running below capacity on return routes. Supply framing: "we have empty trucks, what freight can we fill them with?" Demand framing: "are there shippers with freight that fits this route profile: timing, volume, handling requirements?" The trucks being empty doesn't create demand. The question is whether a real workflow match exists.

**Make assumptions explicit.**
Every team has unspoken assumptions about who the customer is, what they want, and why they'll pay. Those assumptions diverge across people silently. Surfacing them doesn't require having answers, it requires honesty about what you don't know.

**Pressure-test the picture, not just the assumption.**
A stated assumption can look reasonable while being downstream of a wrong mental model. When reviewing assumptions, ask not just "is this testable?" but "what picture of the world generated this assumption, and is that picture accurate?" A bad mental model silently corrupts every assumption built on it.

**Questions before data.**
Raise the questions first, then go collect data. Data collection is hard work and takes time. You want to be gathering only the most important data most relevant to what you're trying to answer.

**Coalition alignment is a precondition.**
Strategy must be developed with the right people in the room. Without that, you go in opposite directions regardless of how good the analysis is.

**Sequence research: internal, then primary, then secondary.**
Start with internal field experts, not organizational leaders who will delegate the question, but the people closest to the customer and the problem. Five or six is usually enough to get clear signal. Then move to integrators and partners. Secondary research (industry reports, market data) comes last, and for niche markets often doesn't exist at all, don't expect it to. AI-assisted research can surface forum commentary and online discussion, but primary conversations are the data.

**Deviant signal handling.**
A single outlier signal doesn't reopen a question that the rest of the fieldwork has closed. When you encounter one, examine the source's expertise and experience relative to the specific domain being assessed, not their general credibility. The deviation may still be worth noting as nuance, it just doesn't carry the same weight as a demand-side signal from a commercial contact.

**When fieldwork surfaces an adjacent signal.**
Sometimes an investigation into hypothesis A produces strong, consistent signal for hypothesis B. Don't fold B into the A investigation, it muddies both. Close A cleanly with a recommendation, use what you found to point toward B, then start a fresh investigation for B. This keeps the original mandate crisp and gives the new thread the proper framing it deserves.

**Frameworks are tools, not answers.**
Business canvas, lean canvas, Play to Win, each has a purpose and is context-dependent. Misuse comes from treating them as templates to fill out rather than as lenses to see through.
