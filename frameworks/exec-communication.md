# Executive Communication

Principles and rituals for communicating upward: with managers, directors, VPs, and C-level. Apply when preparing for executive meetings, writing pre-reads, or reviewing upward-facing documents.

---

## Core principles

**1. Treat the executive as a user**
Apply the same curiosity and empathy you use in product discovery. Their calendar is a strobe light, they haven't thought about your problem since your last meeting. Your job is to help them get there, not assume they're ready.

**2. Recommendation first, always**
In writing and in speech: lead with the answer. Context, process, and evidence come after. If they agree with the recommendation, they may not need the rest.

**3. Go in to learn, not to convince**
The goal of the meeting is not approval, it's co-creation. Executives who feel like you want their input (not their rubber stamp) give better input. The frame shift: *what can I extract from someone who knows things I don't?*

**4. Present options, not one solution**
Arriving with a single recommendation signals you may have missed something. Arriving with three options, and a clear point of view on which is best, signals you did the work. The middle option often wins by design (Goldilocks effect).

**5. Pick up on subtle threads quickly**
"I wonder if we should..." or "Have you considered..." are invitations, not passing comments. The best PMs act on these fast. Speed of response to a subtle thread is what separates senior from junior behavior.

**6. Kill things to build trust**
Deprioritizing something, especially something you worked on, is a senior signal. It shows your incentives are aligned with the company, not your project. This earns credit for when you need investment in something new.

**7. Ask about urgency before acting**
Not every exec comment is a mandate. When you hear something that could be an ask: *"How strongly do you feel about this? Is this more important than what we're currently prioritizing?"* This prevents over-rotating on passing thoughts.

## Pre-meeting ritual (2 min)

Before any meeting with someone above you, answer these three questions:

1. What do I want this person to decide or understand by the end?
2. Will I open with that?
3. What is the biggest pressure or concern on their plate right now, and does my ask connect to it?

**Opening frame (first 60 seconds of the meeting):**
> "We're here to discuss [X]. Last time we spoke, we left off at [Y]. Today's goal is [Z]. Is there anything else you were hoping to cover?"

Then stop. Do not fill the silence.

---

## Post-meeting ritual (1 min)

After any meeting with someone above you, answer:

1. Did I go in to learn, or to convince?
2. Was there a thread dropped that I should follow up on?
3. What's the fastest follow-up I can send today?

---

## On building trust over time

- **Act on feedback quickly.** If something was discussed, follow up before the exec has moved on, a week is too late.
- **Shrink the change.** If an idea seems big or risky, propose a small experiment first. A two-week proof of concept is easier to say yes to than a six-month roadmap.
- **Share what failed.** Being honest about what isn't working, before being asked, builds more trust than protecting an idea that's struggling.
- **Show peripheral vision.** Think beyond your product area. Connect your work to company goals, board pressures, and cross-functional dynamics. This is how you show up as strategic, not just executional.
